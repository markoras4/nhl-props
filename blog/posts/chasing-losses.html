After a bad night, the next one starts to feel like a chance to fix it. Stake a bit more, win it back, and the bad night never happened. After two bad nights, a bit more again. Almost every bettor has done this at least once, and plenty of systems sold online are built on it.

It has a name: chasing losses. This week our own track record gave us a clean way to show what it does, because this was the worst week the best picks have had since the record started.

The week

On the evening of November 14 the best picks on the All tab were 10.45 units up after three weeks. Over the six slates from November 15 to 21 the same picks went 16 wins from 93, for a loss of 33.91 units. The record now stands at 23.46 units down from 382 graded picks.

That is a bad week, but not a strange one. At their prices those 93 picks should have produced about 25 winners, and the model expected 29. Sixteen is a long way short of both. It is also the kind of shortfall that turns up from time to time when the average price is above 5.00 and three picks in four are expected to lose. We covered why a record this young can't tell a good model from a lucky one in Fifteen Nights, Two Stories. This post is about something else: what you do with your stakes when a week like this arrives.

A simple chasing rule

Take the most common version. You bet the same picks we did, one unit each. After every losing night you double your stake per pick for the next night. After a winning night you go back to one unit. The idea is that the first good night pays back everything at once.

We replayed that rule on the exact picks, prices and results of the All tab from October 25 to November 21. Nothing else changed.

Flat stakes against doubling after a losing night

For three weeks, chasing looked brilliant. By November 14 the doubling bettor was 163.2 units up while the flat bettor was 10.45 up. Every bad night had been followed, sooner or later, by a good one at a bigger stake. It is exactly the experience that convinces people the method works.

Then came the week. All six slates finished in the red, two of them by only 0.05 units, which under this rule still counts as a loss and still doubles the stake. The stake per pick went 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and finally 32 units on November 21, a 28-pick slate. That night alone put 896 units at risk, and it lost 206.0 of them. The doubling bettor finished at 147.1 units down, a fall of 310 units in seven days.

A milder rule, raising the stake by half instead of doubling it, follows the same path at a smaller size: 65.6 up on November 14, 39.4 down a week later.

What doubling actually changes

Doubling after a loss feels like it improves your odds. It doesn't touch them. Each pick still wins exactly as often as it was going to, and each unit staked still returns what it was going to on average. Over the whole period our picks returned −6.1% per unit with flat stakes. With doubling they returned −6.7%. Practically the same bets, practically the same return per unit.

What changes is how many units you stake, and when. Doubling put 2,207 units through the same 382 picks, almost six times as much, and it put the biggest stakes on the nights straight after a run of losses. If the picks have an edge, that is a riskier way to collect it. If they don't, it is a faster way to lose.

Chasing doesn't change the odds of any bet. It only changes how much you have riding on them when things are going badly.

40,000 replays

One week can be an unlucky example, so we ran the same picks again 40,000 times. In each replay every pick won only as often as its price implies, which is a bettor with no edge at all, and we tracked the flat bettor and the doubling bettor side by side.

This is why chasing is so persuasive. In most replays the doubling bettor ends up ahead. Lots of small wins, collected after bad nights, add up. But the slides are three times as deep in a typical run, and in one replay in twenty the doubling bettor finished more than 516 units down. About four times in ten, someone who started with a 100-unit bankroll would have run through all of it at some point along the way, against three times in a thousand on flat stakes. The method trades a high chance of a small win for a real chance of a loss you can't come back from.

In the replays the stake per pick reached 16 units in a typical run and 256 units in one run in twenty. Real bookmakers limit stakes, and real people run out of money, long before that. The sequence of wins that would have paid it all back usually arrives after the money is gone.

Why it feels right anyway

Part of it is the "he's due" instinct turned on yourself. A bad week does not make a good week more likely. The picks on November 22 have the same chances whether the last seven nights went well or badly.

The other part is the scoreboard in your head. A loss feels like a hole that has to be filled, and the faster the better. But nothing about the next bet knows about the hole. The sensible question before any bet is the same on a good week and a bad one: is this price worth it at this stake? How much you are down is not part of the answer.

What to do in a bad week

  • Keep the stake where it was. Choose a stake you could live with through a long losing run, and leave it there. Our flat record lost 33.91 units this week. If that would hurt, the stake is too big, and raising it would make it worse.
  • Never size a bet to win back a loss. The size of a bet should come from the price and your bankroll, not from yesterday.
  • Set a limit before the week starts. Decide in advance how much you are prepared to lose in a week or a month, and stop when you reach it.
  • Be wary of any system that wins small and often. Doubling schemes look excellent until the run that breaks them. Ours looked excellent for 21 nights.

Our record will keep showing the picks at one unit each, through weeks like this one and the better ones. That is the honest way to measure them, and it is also the only staking we would call responsible.

And if you notice yourself raising stakes to win back what you lost, take it seriously. It is one of the clearest early signs that betting has stopped being fun. Take a break, use your bookmaker's deposit limits, or talk to someone; the help lines are at the bottom of every page on this site.
