The police radios in Detroit carried the news. “Attention all cars,” went the call on the night of November 10, 1963, according to the Detroit Free Press. “Gordie Howe's new record is 545.” Sixty-three years ago tomorrow, in the middle of a game the Red Wings were already winning, a 35-year-old right winger from Saskatchewan became the greatest goal scorer the NHL had ever seen, and he did it while his team was a man short.

It is easy to forget how heavy that number was. Five hundred and forty-four had been Maurice Richard's. Not just a total, a monument. And Howe had been standing next to it for two weeks, unable to move.

The Rocket's mark

Richard got to the top in a way only he could. On November 8, 1952, he scored his 325th career goal in a 6–4 win over Chicago at the Forum, passing Nels Stewart, who had held the record since retiring in 1940. It came ten years to the day after his first NHL goal. Richard was the first player to reach 500, in October 1957 against Glenn Hall, and when he retired in 1960 he had 544 regular-season goals.

That was supposed to last. A lot of people in Montreal thought it would last forever. Three years later, it was being hunted by a man who played a different kind of game entirely: quieter, longer, and much harder to get away from.

Two weeks at 544

Howe tied the record on October 27, 1963, beating Gump Worsley. Then the goal stopped coming. TIME described the stretch that followed the way players tend to remember slumps: defenders leaning on him harder than usual, and teammates passing up their own chances to get him the puck. Detroit lost three of five during the wait and slid to fourth place.

He was not a young man waiting for his moment. He was in his 18th season with the Red Wings, and he already held the NHL records for games, assists and points. This was the one left. Howe knew it, Detroit knew it, and every photographer who could get a credential knew it too.

“Now I can start enjoying life again.”Gordie Howe, after goal No. 545

Shorthanded at the Olympia

The Canadiens came to Olympia Stadium on November 10, which felt about right. If the record was going to fall, it might as well be in front of Montreal, with Charlie Hodge in the opposite net and the Rocket's old team on the other bench.

Detroit led 2–0 in the second period when Alex Faulkner took a major penalty at 13:57. So the Red Wings went down a man for five minutes, the worst possible moment to chase history. About a minute later, Billy McNeill picked up the puck inside his own blue line and took it up the ice with Howe trailing him, yelling at him to keep going. On a three-on-two, McNeill drew Hodge out and slid it across. Howe's wrist shot went low past the goaltender and in beside the post, at 15:06 of the second.

McNeill said later that he did not realize it was the big goal until the crowd began to yell. Howe's answer about the shot was shorter: “That one had to go in.”

How long the record stayed put

Years each man has held the NHL's career goals record since Nels Stewart retired in 1940, counted from the night he passed the last holder.

Ten minutes

The 15,027 people in the building would not let the game go on. They cheered and chanted his name for about ten minutes, while the public-address announcer read out the record and litter came down onto the ice. Both teams waited at their benches. Out in the city, the police radios were passing the word along.

The Red Wings finished the job 3–0. Terry Sawchuk, who had quietly made 39 saves, recorded his 94th career shutout that night, tying George Hainsworth for the most in NHL history. On almost any other evening that would have been the story. Sid Abel, Detroit's coach and general manager, cared about something simpler. “I'm sure it took the heat off him,” he said. Howe said he felt ten pounds lighter.

The Canadiens, to their credit, later made a presentation to him for the goal that took the mark away from their own legend.

“Attention all cars, Gordie Howe's new record is 545.”Detroit police radio, as reported by the Detroit Free Press

A record that kept going

The strange part is what happened next, which is that Howe kept going for a very long time. He played 26 NHL seasons in all and finished with 801 goals. The Rocket's total, the one that had seemed so final, ended up being a stop on the way. Howe held the record for more than thirty years.

When it finally went, it went in Los Angeles. On March 23, 1994, Wayne Gretzky scored his 802nd goal against the Vancouver Canucks at the Great Western Forum, with Kirk McLean out of his net. The Kings lost that game 6–3, which rarely gets mentioned. Howe, who was not there, was gracious about it: “If someone had to bump me down the ladder, I'm happy that it was him.”

Gretzky held the record for 31 years and finished with 894, and most people assumed that one was final too. Then on April 6, 2025, on a power play at UBS Arena, Alex Ovechkin beat Ilya Sorokin for his 895th. The Capitals lost that night as well, 4–1. Gretzky was asked about it and offered the line that everyone in that chain could have said in their own time: “They say records are made to be broken, but I'm not sure who's going to get more goals than that.”

Why it still matters

Since Stewart retired in 1940, only four men have held this record. Each of them took it from someone who seemed unreachable, and two of them passed it in a game their team went on to lose, because hockey has never been very interested in timing a good story.

Howe's version was different. It came on a shorthanded rush, in a shutout, in his own building, with the whole city listening in. There are bigger numbers now. But it's hard to picture a police dispatcher announcing them.
